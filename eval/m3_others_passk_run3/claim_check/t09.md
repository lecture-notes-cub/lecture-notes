# Small-Sample Limitations of Maximum Likelihood Estimation

## Key Example: Two-Heads Coin

The lecture uses a simple coin-flipping example to illustrate how maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) can produce counterintuitive results with small sample sizes.

### Problem Setup
- **Observation**: Toss a coin `n` times, observe `k` heads
- **MLE Estimate**: `\hat{p} = k/n` (the sample proportion of heads)

### Small Sample Case (n=2)
- **Observation**: 2 heads in 2 tosses
- **MLE Estimate**: `\hat{p} = 2/2 = 1` (100% probability of heads)
- **Common Sense**: This estimate contradicts intuition (e.g., we wouldn't bet $1,000 that the coin is two-headed)
- **Timestamp**: [00:31:05–00:31:38]

### Why This Happens
- MLE properties (e.g., consistency, unbiasedness) hold for large `n` but fail for small `n`
- With small samples, MLE can produce estimates that are statistically implausible
- **Critical Insight**: The example shows that MLE is not always justified for small samples (e.g., `n=2` is too small to trust the estimate)
- **Timestamp**: [00:31:38–00:32:11]

### Contrast with Large Samples (n=1000)
- **Observation**: 1000 tosses, `k` heads
- **MLE Estimate**: `\hat{p} = k/1000`
- **Common Sense**: This estimate aligns with intuition (e.g., 500 heads would suggest ~50% probability of heads)
- **Timestamp**: [00:30:25–00:31:05]

## Why This Matters
- MLE is widely used but has limitations when sample sizes are small
- The example highlights that MLE properties (e.g., consistency) require large `n` to hold
- **Key Takeaway**: Small samples can lead to MLE estimates that contradict real-world expectations
