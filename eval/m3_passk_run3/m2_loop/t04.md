# Marginalization in Probability Theory

## Key Concept: Marginal Distribution

The process of obtaining a distribution over a subset of variables by integrating out the others (marginalization) is fundamental in probability theory.

## Mathematical Derivation

From the lecture transcript:

- **Conditional Density Integration**: The integral of the conditional density $P(y|x)$ with respect to $y$ equals 1, as it represents a probability density function.
  
  [00:09:08–00:10:00]

- **Marginalization Identity**: This leads to the identity:
  $$
  P(x) = \int P(x|y)P(y) \, dy
  $$
  which shows that the marginal distribution $P(x)$ is obtained by integrating the joint distribution over $y$.

- **Expectation Interpretation**:
  [00:11:17–00:12:44]
  The marginal distribution $P(x)$ can be interpreted as the expectation of $P(x|y)$ over all possible $y$ values. This means we average the conditional distributions $P(x|y)$ across all $y$ to obtain the unconditional distribution $P(x)$.

- **Generalization**:
  By induction, this process extends to multiple variables. For example, to obtain the marginal distribution over $x_1, x_2, ..., x_k$ from a joint distribution over $x_1, ..., x_n$, we integrate out the remaining variables.

## Example

The lecture explains that if you know all conditional distributions $P(x|y)$ for different $y$, you can compute the marginal distribution $P(x)$ by averaging these conditional distributions over $y$.

> **Note**: The transcript does not explicitly use the term 'marginalization' but describes the process of marginalization through integration. The term 'expectation interpretation' is clearly discussed as the averaging of conditional distributions.
