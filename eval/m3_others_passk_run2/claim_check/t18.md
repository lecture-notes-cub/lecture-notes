# Bayesian Reasoning Example: Radio Evidence and Model Misspecification

## Context
- The speaker uses a scenario where they compute the probability of a thief being in their house based on:
  - An alarm system that sent an SMS
  - A radio message that announced an earthquake

## Key Calculations
- The posterior probability of a thief being in the house (given the radio message about an earthquake) was calculated to be **~2%** ([01:25:56–01:26:22])
- This calculation used joint probability over variables T (thief), E (earthquake), R (radio), A (alarm)
- The speaker notes they don't actually know if an earthquake occurred, only that the radio announced one ([01:25:56–01:26:22])

## Model Misspecification
- The speaker's model assumed **independence** between earthquakes and thieves ([01:27:07–01:27:44])
- In reality, there's a dependency: thieves listen to the radio and increase activity during earthquakes ([01:27:44–01:28:13])
- This dependency means: P(thief | earthquake) > P(thief | no earthquake)
- The model was too optimistic because it didn't account for this real-world relationship ([01:28:13–01:28:44])

## Real-World Implication
- The speaker's apartment was robbed later, which the speaker attributes to the model misspecification ([01:26:23–01:26:51])
- The model was correct in Bayesian inference but incorrect in formalization (causal graph)

## Why This Matters
- This example shows how Bayesian reasoning can extend classical Boolean logic ([01:28:44–01:29:28])
- Model misspecification can lead to incorrect predictions even when Bayesian inference is applied correctly
- Real-world dependencies (like crime activity increasing after accidents) must be incorporated into models
