# Marginalization and Expectation Interpretation

## Definition
The marginal distribution of a random variable $x$ is obtained by integrating out all other variables from the joint distribution. For a bivariate case, this means:

$$P(x) = \int P(x|y)P(y) \, dy$$

This expression is derived from the law of total probability and represents the expectation of $P(x|y)$ with respect to $y$.

## Expectation Interpretation
The marginal distribution $P(x)$ can be interpreted as the expectation of the conditional distribution $P(x|y)$ over all possible values of $y$:

$$P(x) = \mathbb{E}_y [P(x|y)]$$

This means that to obtain the unconditional distribution of $x$, we average all conditional distributions $P(x|y)$ across the distribution of $y$.

## Physical Meaning
As described in the lecture, this process has a clear physical interpretation: if you know all conditional distributions $P(x|y)$ for arbitrary $y$, you can obtain the unconditional distribution $P(x)$ by taking the expectation over $y$.

## Example
For a joint distribution $P(x, y)$, marginalizing over $y$ gives:

$$P(x) = \int P(x, y) \, dy = \int P(x|y)P(y) \, dy$$

This is equivalent to integrating out $y$ from the joint distribution.

## Generalization
By induction, this process extends to multiple variables. For $n$ random variables $x_1, x_2, ..., x_n$, the marginal distribution of $x_1$ can be obtained by integrating out $x_2, ..., x_n$ from the joint distribution.
