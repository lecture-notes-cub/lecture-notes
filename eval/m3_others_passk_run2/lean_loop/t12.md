# Sample Size vs. Parameter Count

## Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and Sample Size

- **Key Condition for MLE Validity**: MLE is justified when $n$ (sample size) is *not much smaller than* $d$ (dimension of parameters), i.e., $n \gg d$. When $n$ is comparable to $d$, MLE becomes unstable and leads to overfitting. [00:40:46–00:41:49]

- **Overfitting in Practice**:
  - When $n \approx d$ (e.g., training deep neural networks with small models on large datasets), the model fits the training data well but generalizes poorly (high validation error). [00:43:19–00:43:49]
  - When $n \gg d$ (e.g., large datasets with simple models), the model generalizes well (low validation error). [00:43:50–00:44:19]

- **Historical Context**:
  - Early deep neural networks (15-20 years ago) had small parameter counts and large datasets, so they did not overfit. [00:44:19–00:44:52]
  - As models grew larger ($d$ increased), overfitting became prevalent due to $n \ll d$. [00:44:52–00:45:20]

## Bayesian Updating with Zero Observations

- **Posterior Distribution at $n=0$**:
  - When there are no observations ($n=0$), the posterior distribution equals the prior distribution. [00:45:58–00:46:28]

- **Why Bayesian Works for $n=0$**:
  - Bayesian methods are defined for any $n \geq 0$.
  - At $n=0$, the posterior is the prior (e.g., uniform distribution for a continuous variable). This reflects the lack of information gained. [00:45:58–00:46:28]

- **Example**:
  - If you have a coin with unknown bias (prior: uniform distribution), and you observe no flips ($n=0$), the posterior is still uniform (no information gained). [00:45:58–00:46:28]

- **Key Distinction from Classical Statistics**:
  - Classical MLE: Point estimate (e.g., maximum likelihood estimate).
  - Bayesian: Distribution of possible parameters (posterior distribution). [00:40:46–00:41:49]
