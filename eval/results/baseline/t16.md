## Alarm System Example: Variables, Causal Graph, and Probabilities

### Variables

- **T** (Thief): Binary variable. 1 = thief present, 0 = no thief.
- **E** (Earthquake): Binary variable. 1 = small earthquake, 0 = no earthquake.
- **R** (Radio message): Binary variable. 1 = radio announces small earthquake, 0 = no radio message.
- **A** (Alarm): Binary variable. 1 = SMS alarm triggered, 0 = no SMS alarm.

> **Note**: The ASR uses "ARM" (likely a typo for "alarm") and "R" for radio message. The lecture clarifies that "R" is the radio message about earthquakes, while "A" is the SMS alarm.

### Causal Graph

The causal graph is defined as:

- **T** and **E** are independent causes of **A**.
- **E** is the sole cause of **R**.

This graph reflects the inductive bias of the problem and simplifies the joint distribution decomposition.

> **Source**: [01:07:15–01:07:47] "So this is my causal graph. I try to understand what may cause what. So my alarm system may work only because of either thief or earthquake. So then I have this causal dependency and radio message about earthquakes may be caused only by the earthquake."

### Assumptions

1. **Independence of T and E**: The presence of a thief and earthquake are independent events.
2. **Causal structure**: 
   - **A** is caused by **T** and **E** (thief or earthquake).
   - **R** is caused only by **E** (earthquake).
3. **Alarm system behavior**:
   - If there is a thief (**T=1**), the alarm **always triggers** (**A=1**).
   - If there is no thief (**T=0**) and no earthquake (**E=0**), the alarm **does not trigger** (**A=0**).
   - If there is an earthquake (**E=1**) and no thief (**T=0**), the alarm **triggers in 10% of cases** (**A=1** with probability 0.1).
