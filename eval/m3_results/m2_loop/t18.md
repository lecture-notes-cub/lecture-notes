# Radio Evidence, Low Burglary Posterior, and Model Misspecification

## Radio Evidence as Bayesian Evidence

The lecturer uses a radio announcement about an earthquake as evidence in a Bayesian calculation. Specifically:

- At [01:23:46–01:24:29], the lecturer states: *"that the thief is in my house based on the alarm system that has sent me SMS and based on radio message that announced about earthquake."

This radio message is treated as evidence in the conditional probability calculation, where the lecturer computes the joint probability over variables T (thief), E (earthquake), R (radio), and A (alarm).

## Low Burglary Posterior

The lecturer calculates a low posterior probability of burglary (thief in house) after incorporating radio evidence:

- At [01:26:23–01:26:51], the lecturer states: *"it appeared that there was just 2% of probability that there was really a thief in my house."

This 2% posterior probability was derived using the joint distribution and sum rules, but the lecturer notes that this low probability was later proven incorrect when the apartment was actually robbed.

## Model Misspecification

The lecturer identifies a critical model misspecification:

- The model assumed independence between earthquakes and thieves, but in reality, there is a dependency:
  - At [01:27:07–01:27:44], the lecturer explains: *"thieves are not idiots. They also listen to the radio. And if they listen that there was a small earthquake, they immediately increase their thief activity because they know that now different orbs that will be generated will be interpreted as a result of a small earthquake rather than as a result of thief penetration."

- This dependency causes the posterior probability to be too low (2%) when the model doesn't account for it.

## Why the Model Was Incorrect

The lecturer explains that the model was too optimistic because:

- At [01:27:44–01:28:13], the lecturer states: *"the probability that there is a thief, if there was, if there was earthquake is larger than probability of thief if there was no earthquake."

- This is an example of how model misspecification can lead to incorrect predictions (the apartment was robbed).

## Key Takeaway

This example demonstrates:

1. The importance of correctly modeling dependencies between variables in Bayesian inference
2. How a low posterior probability (2%) can be misleading if the model doesn't account for real-world dependencies (e.g., thieves responding to radio announcements)
3. The concept of model misspecification where the assumed independence between variables leads to incorrect conclusions

> **Note**: The lecturer explicitly states that *"if my model were correct, then all the deviations are correct as well"* (at [01:28:44–01:29:28]), highlighting that the model misspecification was the root cause of the robbery.
