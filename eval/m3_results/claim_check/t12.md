# Sample Size vs. Parameter Count and Bayesian Updating

## Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and Sample Size

- **Key Insight**: In classical statistics, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is justified when the sample size $n$ is not much larger than the dimension $d$ of the estimated parameters (i.e., $n \gg d$).

  - [00:40:46–00:41:49] MLE is a point estimate that works well when $n$ is large (but not *much* larger than $d$).

  - [00:41:49–00:42:46] When $n$ is not much larger than $d$, MLE can only be treated as a reasonable approximation without theoretical guarantees.

- **Practical Implication**: In machine learning, when training models with $n \approx d$ (number of observations ≈ number of parameters), you immediately observe overfitting (gap between train and validation error). When $n \gg d$, there's no gap (good generalization).

  - [00:43:19–00:43:49] When $n$ is smaller or comparable with $d$, you immediately observe overfitting.

  - [00:43:50–00:44:19] Deep neural networks (15-20 years ago) had small parameter counts and large datasets, so they didn't overfit (MLE was optimal).

  - [00:44:52–00:45:20] As networks grew larger, the condition $n \gg d$ was violated, leading to overfitting (gap between train and validation error).

## Bayesian Updating with No Observations

- **Key Insight**: Bayesian framework works for any number of observations, including $n = 0$.

  - [00:45:58–00:46:28] When $n = 0$, the posterior distribution equals the prior distribution (since no observations have been made).

  - **Example**: If we have no observations, the posterior is uniform (or the distribution of the variable itself) because we have no information.

  - **Update Process**: With one observation, we can update the posterior from the prior.

- **Why This Matters**: Unlike MLE, Bayesian methods don't require $n \gg d$ to work. They provide a natural interpretation of uncertainty even with no data.

## Summary

- **Sample Size vs. Parameter Count**: When $n \gg d$, MLE works well (no overfitting). When $n \approx d$, overfitting occurs.
- **Bayesian Updating**: Works for $n = 0$ (posterior = prior) and provides a natural way to update with observations.
