# Sample Size vs. Parameter Count

## Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and Sample Size

- **Key Condition for MLE Validity** ([00:40:46–00:41:49]): MLE is justified when $n$ (sample size) is *not much smaller than* $d$ (dimension of parameters), i.e., $n \gg d$. When $n$ is comparable to $d$, MLE becomes unstable and leads to overfitting.

- **Overfitting in Practice** ([00:43:19–00:43:49]): When $n \approx d$ (e.g., training deep neural networks with small models on large datasets), the model fits the training data well but generalizes poorly (high validation error).

- **Historical Context** ([00:44:19–00:44:52]): Early deep neural networks (15-20 years ago) had small parameter counts and large datasets, so they did not overfit. As models grew larger ($d$ increased), overfitting became prevalent due to $n \ll d$.

## Bayesian Updating with Zero Observations

- **Posterior Distribution at $n=0$** ([00:45:58–00:46:28]): When there are no observations ($n=0$), the posterior distribution equals the prior distribution.

- **Why Bayesian Works for $n=0$** ([00:45:58–00:46:28]): Bayesian methods are defined for any $n \geq 0$. At $n=0$, the posterior is the prior (e.g., uniform distribution for a continuous variable).

- **Example** ([00:45:58–00:46:28]): If you have a coin with unknown bias (prior: uniform distribution), and you observe no flips ($n=0$), the posterior is still uniform (no information gained).

- **Key Distinction from Classical Statistics** ([00:40:46–00:41:49]): Classical MLE: Point estimate (e.g., maximum likelihood estimate). Bayesian: Distribution of possible parameters (posterior distribution).
